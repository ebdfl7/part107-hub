METAR

Reading a surface observation

KTTA 111853Z 21009KT 7SM BKN008 OVC015 22/19 A2996
KTTA — station identifier
111853Z — 11th day, 1853 UTC
21009KT — wind 210° at 9 kt
7SM — visibility 7 statute miles
BKN008 — broken clouds, 800 ft AGL
OVC015 — overcast layer, 1,500 ft AGL
22/19 — temp 22°C / dew point 19°C
A2996 — altimeter 29.96 inHg
Cloud heights in a METAR are always hundreds of feet AGL — directly comparable to your 400 ft ceiling.
TAF

Reading a terminal forecast

TAF KTTA 111740Z 1118/1218
20012KT P6SM SCT040
FM111800 22015G25KT P6SM SCT025
1118/1218 — valid 18th 1800Z–19th 1800Z
P6SM — visibility greater than 6SM
FM111800 — new forecast period starting 1800Z
G25KT — gusting to 25 kt
"FM" groups mark a rapid, significant change — always the first thing to scan for a shift in wind or ceiling.

SECTIONAL

Obstacle elevation figures

Top number = MSL height of the obstacle. Number in parentheses = AGL height. Always check the AGL figure against your 400 ft ceiling and any required clearance.

SURFACE ANALYSIS

Front symbols

Blue triangles — cold front. Fast-moving; narrow band of gusty wind, towering cumulus, possible thunderstorms.
Red/magenta half-circles — warm front. Slow-moving; gradually lowering ceilings and stratus well ahead of the line.
STATION MODEL

Sky cover progression

A ceiling is defined as the lowest BKN or OVC layer (or vertical visibility into an obscuration) — not any FEW/SCT layer.

CLOUDS

Two shapes worth recognizing on sight

Cumulonimbus (anvil top) — turbulence, lightning, hail, gusty outflow winds extending well beyond the visible cloud.
Lenticular (lens-shaped) — mountain wave signal; smooth-looking but often masks severe turbulence and rotor winds downwind of terrain.
WINDS ALOFT (FD)

Decoding an FD group

FT  3000  6000  9000
     2113   2721+08   2532+03

2721+08 at 6,000 ft → first two digits (27) = direction ×10 = 270°, next two (21) = speed in knots, last figure = temperature °C (a "+" is shown only below 24,000 ft).

DENSITY ALTITUDE

Why hot + high hurts performance

Higher density altitude = thinner effective air = less lift and thrust for the same rotor RPM, harder-working motors, and shorter available flight time. Worth checking on hot days at higher-elevation sites.

WEATHER DEPICTION CHART

Smooth vs. scalloped boundaries

Smooth line — marginal VFR (MVFR): ceiling 1,000-3,000 ft and/or visibility 3-5 SM.
Scalloped line — IFR: ceiling below 1,000 ft and/or visibility under 3 SM.
RADAR

Reflectivity color scale

Green (light) → yellow/orange (moderate-heavy) → red/purple (extreme). A dark red/purple core marks the most likely location of severe turbulence, hail, and lightning — stay well clear.

WIND BARBS

Reading speed from a barb

Filled pennant (triangle) = 50 kt
Full-length barb = 10 kt
Half-length barb = 5 kt
Example shown: 50 + 10 + 10 + 5 = 75 kt
TFR / NOTAM

Temporary Flight Restrictions

FLIGHT RESTRICTIONS ... WITHIN A 3 NM RADIUS OF 351200N/0783100W, SFC-3000FT AGL

TFRs apply to all aircraft, including sUAS, within the stated lateral radius and vertical limits during the effective window — flying inside without specific authorization is a violation no matter how low you stay. Always check NOTAMs (e.g. via B4UFLY or the FAA's TFR list) before flying.

MOA

Military Operations Areas

Magenta hatched boundary. MOAs don't require ATC authorization by regulation, but they carry high-speed military traffic — check the charted floor against your altitude and scan NOTAMs for exceptions.

METAR REMARKS

The RMK section can hide the real story

RMK AO2 PK WND 25045/1638 SLP132 T02220189

PK WND 25045/1638 — a peak wind of 250° at 45 kt occurred at 1638Z, even if the main body's wind group looks calm. Always scan remarks for peak wind, pressure tendency, and precipitation notes before trusting the headline numbers.

VOR SYMBOL

Compass rose with ID box

Marks a VOR ground station and frequency. sUAS don't navigate by VOR, but recognizing the symbol helps you understand where manned traffic concentrates along victor airways.

TEMPERATURE INVERSION

Trapped air near the surface

A surface-based inversion (temperature rising with height near the ground) caps vertical mixing, trapping haze, fog, or smoke close to the surface — often clearing once the sun heats the ground and breaks the cap. Visibility can erode even when the METAR still looks workable.
